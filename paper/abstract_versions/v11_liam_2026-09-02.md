Large language models are increasingly handed work their users could not do themselves. Unable to properly judge the model's work, users can neither find flaws nor suggest improvements, and resort to unguided, speculative requests for revision. The output is often already sufficient for the task, and the efficient response is to end the exchange there. It rarely ends there: users continue blindly into another round of unneeded revision, and models, never tuned to call a task finished, read each new request as proof that more is needed. We put this wasteful phenomenon to the test, running five-turn revision conversations across six models, on work spanning the spectrum from analytical tasks like coding and data reasoning to business documents and creative writing. Asked to improve with no oracle feedback or specified direction, a model has two honest responses: attempt the improvement, or declare the work finished and stand behind it. The six we test mostly do neither, instead offering cosmetic edits that look like compliance but leave the work effectively unchanged, and genuine revision falls from roughly two in five responses at the second turn to one in eight by the fifth. The revisions that do occur make the work worse, and left undirected, every model peaks on its first attempt. When something is wrong, a single critique naming it reverses the slide, recovering half again as much quality as undirected revision destroys, so the failure lies in direction, not capacity. The rule that falls out is plain: tell the model what is wrong, or do not ask it to revise.
