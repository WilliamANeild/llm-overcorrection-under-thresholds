\begin{abstract}
Do user-stated quality thresholds (``this only needs to be a 70 out of 100'') actually constrain LLM revision behavior? In a two-turn writing setup with 3,840 trials (Study~1), we cross follow-up prompt phrasing (2 levels), stated quality threshold (8 levels), and model (Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Flash, GPT-4o) across eight scenarios. Quality thresholds have \textit{no detectable effect} on whether the model revises. A revision-implying follow-up (``Can this be improved?'') triggers near-universal revision (99.9\%) regardless of threshold, while an evaluative follow-up produces 0.3--38.0\% revision rates depending on model. Within triggered revisions, thresholds produce only a weak-to-moderate dose-response (Spearman $\rho = -0.14$ to $-0.50$). These results suggest a two-stage pattern: prompt phrasing controls whether the model revises; the stated threshold modulates revision intensity only after revision is triggered. A momentum experiment (Study~2, 1,728 trials) shows that prior revision rounds shift the gate in a model-specific way: GPT-4o jumps from 31\% to 98\% revision with a single prior round, while Claude and Gemini resist. A multi-turn revision yield experiment (Study~3, 720 trials, 6 models, 5 turns) tests downstream consequences: when models are given repeated balanced revision opportunities, output quality drops from 4.34 to 2.45 on a six-level scale, with 64.3\% of revisions occurring after the output was already rated sufficient by a blind evaluator. Five of six models degrade; a cohort-locked balanced panel confirms that 65\% of the decline is genuine within-trial degradation, not compositional artifact. Models recognize the problem: 83.7\% of the time they prefer their Turn~1 output when freed from conversational pressure. Targeted feedback closes the gap, outperforming generic revision probing by 2.0 quality levels ($p < 0.0001$), showing that the problem is not revision capacity but undirected revision triggered by conversational pragmatics.
\end{abstract}
