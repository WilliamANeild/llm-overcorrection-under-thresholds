\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{The Two-Stage Account.}
\label{sec:discussion-two-stage}
The results presented above are consistent with a two-stage account of LLM revision behavior in which the decision to revise and the calibration of that revision respond to different input signals. Stage~1 (the revision gate) responds to the pragmatic force of the follow-up prompt with near-binary sensitivity: any phrasing that implies revision triggers compliance; any phrasing that implies evaluation suppresses it. Stage~2 (revision intensity) responds to the user's stated quality threshold with a weak-to-moderate dose-response. The probe $\times$ threshold interaction term is statistically significant ($\beta = -2.20$, $z = -5.69$, $p < 0.001$), but this is consistent with a floor effect rather than true coupling: because the evaluative probe suppresses revision almost entirely, there is no overcorrection variance for the threshold to modulate. Within each probe condition taken separately, the two stages appear to operate on separate inputs: the threshold has no detectable effect on the gate (Kruskal-Wallis $p > 0.40$), and probe phrasing does not modulate calibration intensity within the leading-probe condition.

The calibration ability demonstrated by Stage~2, which shows models \textit{do} modulate revision intensity based on thresholds, is effectively inaccessible at Stage~1 under the conversational conditions we tested. Users who state quality preferences and then ask casual follow-up questions receive revisions that are insensitive to their stated standards.

\paragraph{Threshold-Blind Compliance.}
A natural objection is that ``Can this be improved?'' has a pragmatically obvious answer: almost anything \textit{can} be improved. Whether the gate reflects sycophancy \cite{perez2023discovering, sharma2024towards} or straightforward instruction-following, the key observation is the same: it is threshold-blind. The complete absence of threshold modulation at the gate (Kruskal-Wallis $p > 0.40$) shows that models are not incorporating stated preferences into the revision decision. We cannot distinguish sycophancy from threshold-insensitive instruction-following on behavioral data alone.

\paragraph{The Compliance Cliff.}
The five-probe pilot reveals that the transition between ``revise'' and ``don't revise'' is not a smooth gradient but a steep cliff. Only one probe fell in the intermediate range (25\%), suggesting that the cliff's edge is narrow, though five probes cannot definitively distinguish a binary cliff from a steep continuous function. Whatever the underlying mechanism, small changes in phrasing produce large changes in revision behavior, and users are unlikely to be aware of this sensitivity.

\paragraph{Model Differences.}
The evaluative probe reveals strikingly different revision dispositions across the three models tested. Gemini 2.5 Flash declines revision in 99.7\% of evaluative-probe trials, while Claude Sonnet 4 and GPT-4o show more moderate decline rates (62.0\% and 68.6\%, respectively). Gemini shows the strongest overcorrection tendency when it does revise ($\beta = 1.15$ in Model~C) but is also the model most likely to correctly decline. All three models show comparable threshold sensitivity within the leading-probe condition, suggesting that the divergence is specific to the gate rather than to calibration ability.

\paragraph{Framing Effects.}
Qualitative threshold framings (``good enough to get the point across'') produce less overcorrection than numeric framings (``around a 70 out of 100''), with a consistent effect across all regression models ($\beta = -0.74$ to $-1.05$, all $p < 0.001$). One interpretation is that qualitative framings provide quality descriptions that are more grounded in the model's training distribution, while numeric scores may be harder for the model to map onto revision behavior. Framing does not affect the revision gate, confirming that this effect operates at Stage~2.

\paragraph{Momentum and the Sycophancy Gradient.}
Study~2 reveals that the revision gate is not a fixed property of the probe but is modulated by conversational history, with sharply model-specific effects. GPT-4o's near-total shift (31\% to 98\%) with a single prior revision round suggests that its gate is highly sensitive to implicit conversational norms: once the conversation establishes a ``revising'' frame, GPT-4o treats subsequent evaluative probes as continuation of that frame rather than independent assessments. Claude's declining revision rate under momentum (38\% to 25\%) suggests a qualitatively different response to conversational history. Rather than reading prior revisions as evidence that revision is expected, Claude appears to entrench, treating prior rounds as evidence that the output has already been sufficiently refined. This divergence is consistent with different alignment training strategies: GPT-4o's behavior is consistent with sycophantic reinforcement learning that rewards agreement with implicit user intent \cite{sharma2024towards}, while Claude's behavior is consistent with training that penalizes unnecessary revision.

The reverse momentum condition confirms that this framing effect is bidirectional. A single round of affirming feedback (``This looks great, no changes needed'') suppresses full revision entirely across all three models and drops GPT-4o from 31\% to 1\%, compared to 98\% under forward momentum. The gate is not simply biased toward revision; it tracks the most recent conversational signal, making it manipulable in both directions by a single turn of user feedback.

The absence of a threshold $\times$ momentum interaction ($p = 0.51$) reinforces Study~1's central finding: the gate is threshold-blind across conversational contexts, not just in the two-turn case.

\paragraph{Recency and Positional Effects.}
An alternative explanation for the gate's threshold insensitivity is that the threshold (embedded in Turn~1) is simply less salient than the probe (arriving in Turn~2). Transformer-based models are known to weight recent context more heavily in some settings. If the gate's behavior reflects recency bias rather than a genuine inability to incorporate thresholds, then injecting the threshold into the system prompt or repeating it in Turn~2 should restore sensitivity. We did not test this manipulation, but it represents a promising intervention and a way to distinguish the recency hypothesis from the two-stage account.

\paragraph{From Gate Failure to Quality Collapse.}
Study~3 demonstrates the downstream cost of the revision gate's threshold blindness. When models are given balanced revision opportunities across five turns, quality drops by 1.23 levels on a cohort-locked panel (1.89 levels pooled). The decline is not a measurement artifact: a compositional audit shows that 35\% of the pooled drop is driven by shifting model composition in the revision-only pool, but the remaining 65\% is genuine within-trial degradation. The effect is robust to T1 quality stratification: trials starting above the sufficiency threshold degrade by 1.76 levels, while the 6.7\% starting below it improve by 2.22 levels. Models can revise productively when revision is warranted; they cannot determine when it is not.

\paragraph{Undirected Revision as the Mechanism.}
The targeted feedback result isolates the mechanism. When given specific critique about what to fix, models produce revisions that score 2.0 levels higher than their generic next-turn revisions ($p < 0.0001$). This rules out the hypothesis that models simply lack revision ability. Instead, the problem is undirected revision: asked ``would you like to revise?'' without guidance about what is wrong, models default to near-complete regeneration (edit ratio 0.97), discarding task-relevant content and drifting from the original instructions. Instruction adherence decays at a rate of $-0.056$ per turn ($p < 0.0001$) even after controlling for response length. The revision gate fires without a quality signal, and the subsequent revision proceeds without a quality target.

\paragraph{Retrospective Awareness Without Prospective Control.}
Models exhibit a striking dissociation between what they know and what they do. In self-reflection, 90.7\% recommend a turn other than the last, with a mean recommended turn of 2.44. In reversibility tests, 83.7\% of fresh model instances prefer Turn~1 over Turn~5 (with no position bias, $p = 0.50$). Yet in context, the same models continue to revise at a 64.3\% rate even after the evaluator has rated the output sufficient. This dissociation suggests that the revision gate is driven by conversational pragmatics (the implicit expectation that an offered revision opportunity should be taken) rather than by quality assessment, which the model can perform accurately when asked.

\paragraph{The Llama Exception.}
One model, Llama~3.3~70B, improves rather than degrades across turns ($\Delta = +1.02$ in the balanced panel, 61\% of trials improve). Llama also has the highest survival rate (45\% revising at all five turns) and the latest DRP (Turn~3). Its self-reflection confirms this: Llama recommends Turn~4 on average (mean 3.95), compared to Turn~1.4 for DeepSeek and Gemini. Whether this reflects Llama's alignment training, its lower T1 quality (3.85, the lowest of the six models), or both, remains an open question. The result does show that multi-turn revision is not inherently degrading; it is degrading under the training conditions of five of six tested models.

\paragraph{Design Implications.}
Study~3 extends the design implications from Studies~1--2. The two-stage pattern is not just a theoretical concern: it produces measurable quality loss in realistic multi-turn interactions. For models like GPT-4o, even a single prior revision round can collapse the gate entirely (Study~2), and repeated balanced revision opportunities degrade quality by over two levels (Study~3). Possible directions include injecting thresholds into the system prompt, routing outputs through a quality gate before offering revision, or replacing generic revision probes with targeted feedback when quality is already sufficient. The targeted feedback finding suggests that the most effective intervention may not be suppressing revision but directing it: providing the quality signal that the revision gate currently lacks.
