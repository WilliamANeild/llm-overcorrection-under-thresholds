% ============================================================
% VARIANT A — light formalism (goes in Methods, "Design" or "Metrics")
% ============================================================
We index the turns of a conversation by $i \in \{1, \dots, 5\}$, where $i = 1$ is the model's
initial output and each subsequent turn $i > 1$ is an undirected revision request. Let $Q_m(i)$
denote the expected quality of model $m$'s turn-$i$ output on the evaluator's five-level scale, and
define the quality-optimal stopping point
\[
  i^{*}_m \;=\; \arg\max_{i} \; Q_m(i).
\]
A model that benefits from revision would have $Q_m$ non-decreasing in $i$ up to a plateau, so that
$i^{*}_m$ marks the turn past which further revision is unnecessary. Our central quantitative finding
is that $Q_m$ is instead decreasing in $i$ for every model we test, so that $i^{*}_m = 1$ throughout:
the quality-maximizing number of undirected revisions is zero. We report the per-turn decline
$Q_m(1) - Q_m(5)$ (the \emph{revision cliff}) and the tokens generated after $i^{*}_m$ (the
\emph{revision tax}) as our primary quantities.

% ============================================================
% VARIANT B — prose only (no notation)
% ============================================================
For each model we track output quality across the five turns and identify the turn at which quality
peaks. A model that benefits from revision should improve, or at least hold steady, as the turns
proceed. Instead, quality peaks at the first turn for every model we test and declines with each
subsequent revision, so the quality-maximizing number of undirected revisions to request is none. We
summarize this with two quantities: the drop in quality from the first turn to the fifth, which we
call the revision cliff, and the fraction of output generated after the peak, which we call the
revision tax.
