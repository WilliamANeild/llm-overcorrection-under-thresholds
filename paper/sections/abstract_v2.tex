Self-correction is the practice of improving a large language model's output by asking the model to
revise its own work. Often the user who asks for a revision cannot say what is wrong, so the request
comes down to asking the model to make it better. In this work we study this undirected
revision across six models and forty tasks, running five-turn conversations in which the model is
offered a neutral choice at each turn to keep or revise its output, with no indication of what to
change. We find that models mostly
decline to genuinely revise, returning restatements and refusals that leave the task content
unchanged, and that this deepens as the conversation continues. The revisions they do produce tend to
make an already-adequate output worse, so that for every model the first version is the best one. A
single specific critique reverses the pattern and improves the output, which places the failure in
the absence of direction rather than in the capacity to revise. Measuring this takes care, because
models wrap their revisions in meta-commentary addressed to the user that biases an automated judge
toward the first draft, a bias that fades once the commentary is removed. Intrinsic self-correction,
on its own, is not enough; the direction has to come from outside.
\end{abstract}
