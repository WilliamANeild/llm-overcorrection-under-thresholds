People and language models alike can revise their own work, but whether it improves depends less on
the revising itself than on the feedback that guides it, and above all on whether that feedback comes
from outside. Asked only to make its answer better, with no outside signal about what is wrong, a
model must correct itself on its own, the situation a user creates whenever they cannot say what the
problem is. In this work we study this undirected revision across six models and forty tasks, over
five-turn conversations that let the model keep or revise its output at each turn. We find that
models mostly decline to genuinely revise, returning restatements and refusals that leave the task
content unchanged, and that this deepens as the conversation continues. The revisions they do produce
tend to make an already-adequate output worse, so that for every model the first version is the best
one. A single specific critique reverses the pattern and improves the output, which places the
failure in the absence of direction rather than in the capacity to revise. Measuring this takes care,
because the meta-commentary that models add to revisions biases an automated judge toward the first
draft until it is removed. Intrinsic self-correction, on its own, is not enough; the direction has to
come from outside.
\end{abstract}
