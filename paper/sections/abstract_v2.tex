Correcting one's own mistakes is a routine part of how people write and reason, and self-correction
has a language model do the same, improving its output by revising it rather than starting over. A
person who revises usually knows what to change, but a model is often asked only to make its answer
better, without being told what is wrong, as when the user cannot say what the problem is. In this
work we study this undirected revision across six models and forty tasks, running five-turn
conversations that offer the model a neutral choice at each turn to keep or revise its output. We
find that models mostly decline to genuinely revise, returning restatements and refusals that leave
the task content unchanged, and that this deepens as the conversation continues. The revisions they
do produce tend to make an already-adequate output worse, so that for every model the first version
is the best one. A single specific critique reverses the pattern and improves the output, which
places the failure in the absence of direction rather than in the capacity to revise. Measuring this
takes care, because the meta-commentary that models add to their revisions biases an automated judge
toward the first draft, a bias that disappears once the commentary is removed. Intrinsic
self-correction, on its own, is not enough; the direction has to come from outside.
\end{abstract}
