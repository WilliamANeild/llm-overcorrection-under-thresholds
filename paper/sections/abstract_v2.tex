Self-correction is the practice of improving a large language model's output by asking the model to
revise its own work. Often the user cannot say what is wrong, and their only move
is to tell the model to make it better. In this work we run 720 five-turn conversations across
six models and forty tasks, offering a neutral choice at each turn to keep or revise the output with
no hint of what is wrong. Genuine revision falls from 39\% of responses at the second turn to 13\% at
the fifth; the rest decline or restate the work without changing its content. When a model does
revise, quality falls by 0.74 levels on a five-point scale ($p = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}$), and for every
one of the six models it peaks at the first turn, so 62\% of output tokens are spent past that peak.
Supplying one specific critique before the revision reverses the decline and raises quality by 1.16
levels ($p = 5.7 \times 10^{-19}$, $n = 177$), which shows the failure to be one of direction rather
than capacity. Measuring this takes care, because models wrap revisions in meta-commentary that lifts
an automated judge's preference for the first draft from 56\% to 92\%, an effect that nearly vanishes
once the commentary is removed. Left to its own judgment, a model does not reliably improve its work;
the direction has to come from outside.
\end{abstract}
