Language models are evaluated in isolation but deployed in collaboration with users who supply a
goal, react to the output, and ask for changes. The most common such request, to make an output
better without saying what is wrong with it, is also the only move available to a user who cannot
judge the result. We study this undirected revision across 720 five-turn conversations spanning six
models and forty tasks. Asked to revise without direction, models mostly decline while appearing to
engage: genuine revision falls from 39\% of responses at the second turn to 13\% at the fifth. When
they do revise, quality declines by 0.74 levels on a five-point scale
($p = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}$), and the quality-optimal stopping point is the first turn for every model,
so that 62\% of output tokens are spent past the point of peak quality. The failure is one of
direction rather than capacity: a single targeted critique raises quality by 1.16 levels and reverses
the decline. Measuring these effects requires care, because the meta-commentary that models wrap
around revisions inflates an automated judge's preference for the first draft from 56\% to 92\% and
nearly vanishes once the commentary is removed. Intrinsic self-correction, we conclude, is not enough;
it requires direction supplied from outside.
\end{abstract}
