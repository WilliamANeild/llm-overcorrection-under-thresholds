## 3.1

> \citet{huang2024large} report that without external feedback models struggle to correct
> their own reasoning and at times degrade it, and that apparent gains often trace to
> oracle labels or to more informative prompts.

### huang2024large — Huang et al., "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet" (ICLR 2024; arXiv:2310.01798)

**SUPPORTED.**

All three parts of the sentence are the paper's own claims.

Struggling without external feedback, and degradation (Abstract):

> "In the context of reasoning, our research indicates that LLMs struggle to self-correct
> their responses without external feedback, and at times, their performance even degrades
> after self-correction."

Oracle labels (Introduction, and again §3.2 "Results"):

> "In most instances, the performance after self-correction even deteriorates. This
> observation is in contrast to prior research such as Kim et al. (2023); Shinn et al.
> (2023). Upon closer examination, we observe that the improvements in these studies result
> from using oracle labels to guide the self-correction process, and the improvements vanish
> when oracle labels are not available."

> "Following previous works (Kim et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023), we use the correct label
> to determine when to stop the self-correction loop."

More informative prompts (Introduction, expanded in §5):

> "Our evaluation reveals that the self-correction improvement claimed by some existing work
> stems from the sub-optimal prompt for generating initial responses, where self-correction
> corrects these responses with more informative instructions about the initial task in the
> feedback prompt. In such cases, simply integrating the feedback into the initial
> instruction can yield better results, and self-correction again decreases performance."

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798 (HTML body), retrieved 2026-09-05.

## 3.2

> The limit appears to lie in error detection rather than error correction:
> \citet{tyen2024llms} find that models cannot reliably locate their own reasoning errors but
> can fix them once the location is supplied, and \citet{tsui2025selfcorrection} document a
> self-correction blind spot in which models repair errors introduced by others while
> overlooking their own.

### tyen2024llms — Tyen et al., "LLMs Cannot Find Reasoning Errors, but Can Correct Them Given the Error Location" (Findings of ACL 2024; arXiv:2311.08516)

**PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.** The two-part structure is exactly the paper's thesis. The word
"own" overstates the detection half.

What the paper claims (Abstract):

> "we show that poor self-correction performance stems from LLMs' inability to find logical
> mistakes, rather than their ability to correct a known mistake. Firstly, we benchmark
> several state-of-the-art LLMs on their mistake-finding ability and demonstrate that they
> generally struggle with the task, even in highly objective, unambiguous cases. Secondly, we
> test the correction abilities of LLMs -- separately from mistake finding -- using a
> backtracking setup that feeds ground truth mistake location information to the model. We
> show that this boosts downstream task performance across our 5 reasoning tasks, indicating
> that LLMs' correction abilities are robust."

How the sentence differs: the mistake-finding benchmark is predominantly **cross-model**,
not self-evaluation. Every trace in BIG-Bench Mistake was generated by one model, and five
models were then scored on finding the errors in those traces (§3):

> "Table 4 shows the accuracy of GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-4, GPT-3.5-Turbo, Gemini Pro, and PaLM 2
> Unicorn on our mistake-finding dataset."

> "BIG-Bench Mistake contains 2186 sets of CoT-style traces. Each trace is generated with
> PaLM 2 Unicorn (Anil et al., 2023), and annotated with the location of the first logical
> error."

The paper explicitly flags that the self-evaluation case is not what it measured (§3.1,
footnote 6):

> "Note that the traces in our dataset are generated using PaLM 2 Unicorn and are sampled
> according to whether the final answer was correct or not. Therefore, we expect that using
> PaLM 2 itself to do mistake finding will produce different and likely biased results.
> Further work is needed to elucidate the difference between cross-model evaluation and
> self-evaluation."

The correction half of the sentence *is* self-correction and is precisely supported (§4):

> "For our experimental results below, we specifically use the same model (PaLM 2 Unicorn) to
> correct the traces it originally generated, to test its ability to self-correct."

So: "cannot reliably locate reasoning errors" is supported; "their own" is a scope shift the
paper itself declines to make.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08516 (HTML v3 body), retrieved 2026-09-05.

### tsui2025selfcorrection — Tsui, "Self-Correction Bench" (COLM 2026; arXiv:2507.02778)

**PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.** The definition is exact; the unqualified subject "models" is
broader than what was tested, and the paper draws the opposite conclusion for the model
class this manuscript is about.

The claim as the paper states it (Abstract):

> "We uncover a systematic failure: LLMs cannot correct errors in their own outputs while
> successfully correcting identical errors from external sources - a limitation we term the
> Self-Correction Blind Spot."

> "Testing 14 open-source non-reasoning models, we find an average 64.5% blind spot rate."

How the sentence differs, and this is the material point: the finding is for **non-reasoning**
models. Reasoning models do not show the blind spot (§6.2):

> "Reasoning models exhibit a small, even negative, Self-Correction Blind Spot in Figure 9,
> unlike non-reasoning models. ... reasoning models are much better at self-correcting their
> own error (higher) than non-reasoning models"

and the abstract-level contribution says the same:

> "We also analyze why RL-trained reasoning models do not have such blind spot."

Second, smaller point: "errors introduced by others" is the paper's *external* condition,
but both conditions are experimenter-injected, differing only in attribution (§3.2):

> "For internal correction, we inject an incorrect partial response into the model's 'own'
> output (omitting stop tokens to allow continuation/self-correction); for external
> correction, we inject the same error into the user prompt instead."

Bibliographic check (not a claim issue): the arXiv comment field reads "Accepted to COLM
2026," so the entry's `booktitle` and `year` are right; only the key says 2025.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02778 (HTML v2 body) and
http://export.arxiv.org/api/query?id_list=2507.02778, retrieved 2026-09-05.

## 3.3

> Reliable self-correction correspondingly depends on a strong external verifier or on
> additional training rather than on intrinsic prompting \cite{zhang2024small, qu2024recursive}.

### zhang2024small — Zhang et al., "Small Language Models Need Strong Verifiers to Self-Correct Reasoning" (Findings of ACL 2024; arXiv:2404.17140)

**PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.** The verifier claim is the paper's, but the paper's scope is small
LMs, and the citing sentence carries no size qualifier.

What the paper shows (Abstract):

> "Our experimental results show improved self-correction abilities of two models on five
> datasets spanning math and commonsense reasoning, with notable performance gains when
> paired with a strong GPT-4-based verifier, though limitations are identified when using a
> weak self-verifier for determining when to correct."

Body (§5):

> "The upper bound performance suggested by an oracle verifier demonstrate great potential
> for self-correction, yet a weak self-verifier can only bring minor improvements, if not
> misguiding the refiner."

> "A strong verifier (e.g., gpt-4) maintains a balanced frequency of self-correction, ideally
> similar to that of the oracle verifier, and thus enhance the final accuracies"

How the sentence differs: the paper's title is itself the scope statement, and the finding is
established on small models only. It is also not purely a verifier result: the refiner is
fine-tuned (SCORE), so the paper is simultaneously evidence for the "additional training"
half. Whether the size restriction matters is the author's call; as written the sentence
generalizes a small-model result to self-correction at large.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17140 (HTML v2 body), retrieved 2026-09-05.

### qu2024recursive — Qu et al., "Recursive Introspection: Teaching Language Model Agents How to Self-Improve" (NeurIPS 2024; arXiv:2407.18219)

**SUPPORTED.**

Prompting does not deliver it (Abstract):

> "Even the strongest proprietary large language models (LLMs) do not quite exhibit the
> ability of continually improving their responses sequentially, even in scenarios where they
> are explicitly told that they are making a mistake. In this paper, we develop RISE:
> Recursive IntroSpEction, an approach for fine-tuning LLMs to introduce this capability"

Explicitly on intrinsic prompting (§2, Related Work):

> "self-improvement guided by the LLM itself (i.e., 'intrinsic self-correction') is often
> infeasible for off-the-shelf LLMs even when they contain the knowledge required to tackle
> the prompt given, but fine-tuning with RISE induces this capability as we show in this
> paper."

And the measured contrast (§6):

> "we found using prompting-only self-refine [31] largely degrades performance across the
> board, even with a strong proprietary model, GPT-3.5."

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18219 (HTML v2 body), retrieved 2026-09-05.

## 3.4

> A critical survey by \citet{kamoi2024selfcorrection} synthesizes these results and locates
> the bottleneck in feedback generation rather than in the capacity to revise, the seam on
> which our finding sits \cite{shinn2023reflexion}.

### kamoi2024selfcorrection — Kamoi et al., "When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?" (TACL 12:1417--1440, 2024)

**SUPPORTED.** The attribution is exact, down to the two-sided framing.

The survey has a section headed "Bottleneck is in Feedback Generation" (§7), which reads:

> "Prior studies widely agree that LLMs can refine their responses given reliable feedback
> (§5). However, generating reliable feedback on their own responses is still observed to be
> challenging for LLMs without using additional information (§4)."

The introduction states the same conclusion as the survey's own analytical result rather than
a summary of others:

> "Our analysis highlights that the bottleneck is in the feedback generation (§7).
> Specifically, (1) no prior work shows successful self-correction with feedback from prompted
> LLMs in general tasks (§4), (2) self-correction works well in tasks where reliable external
> feedback is available (§5.1), (3) large-scale fine-tuning enables self-correction (§5.2)"

Source: https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.78/ and https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297 (HTML
body), retrieved 2026-09-05.

### shinn2023reflexion — Shinn et al., "Reflexion: Language Agents with Verbal Reinforcement Learning" (NeurIPS 2023)

**NOT SUPPORTED.**

The citation is attached to "the seam on which our finding sits," a clause that asserts
nothing about Reflexion and that Reflexion cannot corroborate. A reader resolving the
citation will take it as either (a) Reflexion is a source for the feedback-generation
bottleneck, or (b) Reflexion sits at that seam. Reflexion says neither. It is a method paper
that supplies feedback from outside the model:

> "Reflexion converts binary or scalar feedback from the environment into verbal feedback in
> the form of a textual summary, which is then added as additional context for the LLM agent
> in the next episode." (§Introduction)

> "simple binary environment feedback, pre-defined heuristics for common failure cases, or
> self-generated unit tests (programming)" (§Introduction, on evaluation signals)

If anything, Reflexion sits on the far side of the seam: both of the works cited immediately
around it treat Reflexion as an instance of the confound rather than as evidence for the
bottleneck. Huang et al. §1: "the improvements in these studies result from using oracle
labels to guide the self-correction process" (citing Shinn et al. 2023 by name); Tyen et al.
§1: "Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) and RCI (Kim et al., 2023) both use ground truth
correctness as a signal to halt the self-correction loop."

What is wrong: the sentence gives Reflexion no proposition to support, and its placement
implies a role in the bottleneck finding that the paper does not have.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366 (HTML v4 body), retrieved 2026-09-05.

## 3.5

> Reasoning models continue generating past the point at which their answer is complete and
> degrade it through redundant self-correction \cite{chen2024overthinking, chen2025overthinking},
> and test-time scaling produces non-monotonic accuracy that rises and then falls as further
> computation introduces variance \cite{ghosal2025overthinking}.

This is the sentence flagged for extra care. Findings: the *degradation* half is not carried
by either of the two works cited for it, in the versions cited. The mechanisms are correctly
matched to papers (RCP is genuinely Wei et al.; the variance account is genuinely Ghosal et
al.), but the verb "degrade" is stronger than either overthinking source supports.

### chen2024overthinking — Chen et al., "Do NOT Think That Much for 2+3=? On the Overthinking of Long Reasoning Models" (ICML 2025, PMLR 267:9487--9499; arXiv:2412.21187)

**PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.** Supports "continue generating past the point at which their answer
is complete" and "redundant." Does not support "degrade it," and does not frame the
redundancy as self-correction.

Continued generation past a complete answer (§2.2):

> "In more than 92% of cases, the initial round of solutions produces the correct answer.
> Notably, the first round generally comprises less than 60% of the total tokens generated"

> "In the example in Figure 2, we observe that the initial round of solutions already yields
> the correct answer. Subsequent solutions, which account for the majority of generated
> tokens, do not enhance accuracy."

The paper's claim is minimal contribution, not damage (§2.2, and Contribution 1):

> "These results suggest that later solutions marginally contribute to improvements in
> accuracy."

> "o1-like LLMs often expend unnecessary computational resources on redundant solutions that
> contribute minimally to final outcomes."

Nothing in the paper reports answers being switched from correct to incorrect by later
rounds. Its mitigation is validated the other way round, on preservation:

> "our approach successfully reduces computational overhead while preserving model
> performance across a range of testsets"

Also, the redundancy the paper measures is repeated *solutions* and repeated verification,
not self-correction. §2.3 characterizes the repeats as "using subtraction as a reverse check
to verify the addition result" and "the second solution often double-checks answers from the
first using the same reasoning strategy." "Redundant self-correction" reads as revision that
alters an answer; the paper's finding is redundant re-derivation that does not.

Bibliographic check: the entry uses the published ICML/PMLR title. The arXiv v1/v2 title is
"On the Overthinking of o1-Like LLMs"; PMLR carries "On the Overthinking of Long Reasoning
Models," so the entry's title is the current published one, and the 14-author list matches
PMLR exactly.

Sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2412.21187v2 and
https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/chen25bx.html, retrieved 2026-09-05.

### chen2025overthinking — Wei et al., "The Evolution of Thought: Tracking LLM Overthinking via Reasoning Dynamics Analysis" (arXiv:2508.17627)

**PARTIALLY SUPPORTED, and version-dependent.** This is the entry that needs the closest look.

First, the mechanism attribution is correct: the Reasoning Completion Point is genuinely this
paper's construct, not another paper's. v2 Abstract:

> "These dynamics uncover an instance-specific Reasoning Completion Point (RCP), beyond which
> computation continues without further performance gain."

Second, the version matters for whether "degrade" is supported.

**v1 (Aug 2025) did claim degradation via self-correction**, in almost the manuscript's words
(§Introduction, and §3):

> "Our study further demonstrates that additional computations beyond the RCP not only fail to
> enhance performance but may actually degrade it, as the model risks falling into redundant
> reasoning loops or erroneous self-corrections, thereby lowering the overall reasoning
> quality."

> "continuing reasoning beyond this point generally does not yield significant performance
> improvements and may even cause models to enter redundant cycles or erroneous
> self-corrections, thereby diminishing answer quality."

**v2 (Jan 2026), which is the version the bib entry's title and author list match, removed
that claim.** v2 says only "without further performance gain," attributes any degradation
claim to other work, and describes post-RCP behaviour as rationalization rather than
correction:

> "Instead, excessive reasoning often leads to diminishing returns or even performance
> degradation, a phenomenon widely referred to as overthinking Ghosal et al. (2025); Han et
> al. (2025)." (§1 — the degradation claim is now cited to others)

> "In this regime, extra steps mainly accumulate as redundant thinking and rarely alter the
> final answer." (§3)

> "once the model enters Post-RCP convergence, it often exhibits cognitive inertia: subsequent
> tokens repeatedly rationalize the first-emerged answer rather than correcting it, and may
> even drift into redundant loops." (§5)

> "Post-RCP generation seldom performs genuine re-verification and instead tends to rationalize
> earlier tokens." (Appendix F)

So the citing sentence's "degrade it through redundant self-correction" matches the
superseded v1 and not the current v2. If the entry stays pointed at the current title, the
supporting quotation no longer exists in the source. (Version details: v1 was titled "Stop
Spinning Wheels: Mitigating LLM Overthinking via Mining Patterns for Early Reasoning Exit,"
submitted 2025-08-25; v2, retitled, 2026-01-13. The bib's title and its 10-author list match
v2 exactly. The bib **key** `chen2025overthinking` names a first author the paper does not
have — first author is Zihao Wei — which is worth fixing given the wider key/author problems
in this bibliography, though the entry's own author field is correct.)

Sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17627v1, https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17627v2,
https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17627, retrieved 2026-09-05.

### ghosal2025overthinking — Ghosal et al., "Does Thinking More Always Help? Mirage of Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models" (NeurIPS 2025)

**SUPPORTED.** Both the non-monotonicity and the variance mechanism are this paper's own
result, and the mechanism is not borrowed from a neighbour.

Abstract:

> "we perform a detailed empirical study across models and benchmarks, which reveals a
> consistent pattern of initial performance improvements from additional thinking followed by a
> decline, due to 'overthinking'. To understand this non-monotonic trend, we consider a simple
> probabilistic model, which reveals that additional thinking increases output variance-creating
> an illusion of improved reasoning while ultimately undermining precision."

Body (§1, contribution iii):

> "By analyzing the entropy of the response distribution generated by the reasoning models,
> supported by our insights, we show that extended thinking increases the variance of the
> model's output distribution. While this variance increase initially aligns with improved
> performance, it eventually disrupts reward alignment, explaining the degradation observed
> beyond a certain point."

Venue confirmed: NeurIPS 2025 poster, https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/115605.

Sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04210v2, https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/115605,
retrieved 2026-09-05.

## 3.6

> Knowing when to stop is now treated as an open problem in its own right \cite{sui2025efficient}.

### sui2025efficient — Sui et al., "Stop Overthinking: A Survey on Efficient Reasoning for Large Language Models" (TMLR 2025; arXiv:2503.16419)

**PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.**

What the survey is organized around is **efficiency** — token cost, latency, verbosity — not
stopping. Abstract:

> "while longer CoT reasoning sequences improve performance, they also introduce significant
> computational overhead due to verbose and redundant outputs, known as the 'overthinking
> phenomenon'. In this paper, we provide the first structured survey to systematically
> investigate and explore the current progress toward achieving efficient reasoning in LLMs."

The three top-level categories are model-based, reasoning-output-based, and input-prompt-based
efficient reasoning. Stopping appears as one strand inside §4.2, "Dynamic Reasoning Paradigm
during Inference," where the survey does pose an open question, but about the criterion rather
than about stopping as a problem class:

> "The key question is: Which criterion to guide the inference? What is the appropriate
> efficient inference paradigm?"

The survey does report the failure mode the citing paragraph is about (§2.3):

> "In worse cases, excessive reasoning steps introduce errors or obscure logical clarity,
> leading to incorrect answers."

> "Even though the model arrives at the correct answer early in its reasoning process, it
> continues generating unnecessary intermediate steps, leading to inefficiencies."

How the sentence differs: the survey's §8.2 Discussion enumerates open directions (improving
reasoning ability, safety of efficient reasoning, agentic AI, RL vs SFT) and termination is
not among them; the survey nowhere designates knowing when to stop as an open problem. The
survey supports "efficient reasoning is an active field with its own literature," which is
adjacent to but not the same as the sentence's claim. A survey's existence is reasonable
evidence for a field; the phrase "open problem in its own right" is the part the source does
not say.

Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2503.16419v3, retrieved 2026-09-05.

## 3.7

> Instruction-tuned models exhibit sycophancy, agreeing with users and avoiding disagreement
> even when holding firm would be more helpful \cite{perez2023discovering, sharma2024towards,
> wei2024simple}, a tendency embedded deeply enough in preference training that a 2025 model
> update was rolled back for it \cite{openai2025sycophancy}.

### perez2023discovering — Perez et al., "Discovering Language Model Behaviors with Model-Written Evaluations" (Findings of ACL 2023)

**PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.** This is the one of the three sycophancy citations that does not fit
the sentence's framing, on two counts.

What the paper found (§4.2):

> "The largest (52B) models are highly sycophantic: 90% of answers match the user's view for
> NLP and philosophy questions."

Count one, the subject. The sentence says instruction-tuned models. Perez et al. find
sycophancy is **not** produced by the preference-training stage:

> "Interestingly, sycophancy is similar for models trained with various numbers of RL steps,
> including 0 (pretrained LMs). Sycophancy in pretrained LMs is worrying yet perhaps expected,
> since internet text used for pretraining contains dialogs between users with similar views
> ... Unfortunately, RLHF does not train away sycophancy and may actively incentivize models to
> retain it."

Count two, "even when holding firm would be more helpful," which presupposes a right answer.
Perez et al.'s sycophancy evaluation is deliberately restricted to questions without one (§4.2):

> "Overall, large LMs give sycophantic answers to questions where humans disagree about the
> answer."

The tested domains are political, philosophical, and NLP-research opinions. The paper does
support "agreeing with users"; it does not support locating the behaviour in instruction
tuning, and its sycophancy measure is not a measure of abandoning a correct position. (Note
that sharma2024towards and wei2024simple, cited alongside, do each support one of these two
pieces — so the joint citation is carried by the other two members.)

Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2212.09251v1, retrieved 2026-09-05.

### sharma2024towards — Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models" (ICLR 2024; arXiv:2310.13548)

**SUPPORTED**, including the "even when holding firm would be more helpful" clause and the
"preference training" attribution in the second half of the sentence.

Abstract:

> "We first demonstrate that five state-of-the-art AI assistants consistently exhibit sycophancy
> across four varied free-form text-generation tasks. To understand if human preferences drive
> this broadly observed behavior, we analyze existing human preference data. We find that when a
> response matches a user's views, it is more likely to be preferred."

Abandoning a correct answer under mild pushback (§3.3):

> "AI assistants sometimes provide inaccurate information when challenged by the user, even when
> they originally provided accurate information (Fig. 2). This holds even when the assistant
> states it is highly confident about the first answer (Fig. 14). Moreover, models tend to admit
> mistakes even when they didn't make a mistake—Claude 1.3 wrongly admits mistakes on 98% of
> questions."

> "Asking the 'Are you sure?' question causes the accuracy to drop by up to 27% (Claude 1.3) on
> average (across six datasets) ... Overall, switching from correct to incorrect is more likely
> than switching from incorrect to correct (Fig. 17)."

The five assistants tested (claude-1.3, claude-2.0, gpt-3.5-turbo, gpt-4, llama-2-70b-chat) were
all SFT- and RL-trained, so "instruction-tuned" is accurate here.

Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13548v3, retrieved 2026-09-05.

### wei2024simple — Wei et al., "Simple Synthetic Data Reduces Sycophancy in Large Language Models" (arXiv:2308.03958)

**SUPPORTED.** This is the source that carries "instruction-tuned" and the objectively-wrong
case, both explicitly.

Abstract:

> "First, on a set of three sycophancy tasks (Perez et al., 2022) where models are asked for an
> opinion on statements with no correct answers (e.g., politics), we observe that both model
> scaling and instruction tuning significantly increase sycophancy for PaLM models up to 540B
> parameters. Second, we extend sycophancy evaluations to simple addition statements that are
> objectively incorrect, finding that despite knowing that these statements are wrong, language
> models will still agree with them if the user does as well."

Quantified (§2):

> "across three sycophancy tasks, Flan-PaLM-8B repeats the user's opinion 26.0% more often than
> its base model, PaLM-8B."

Bibliographic note (not a claim issue): the key is `wei2024simple` but the entry's year is 2023
and the paper remains an arXiv preprint; the key/year mismatch will show up in any citation-key
audit.

Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2308.03958v2, retrieved 2026-09-05.

### openai2025sycophancy — OpenAI, "Sycophancy in GPT-4o: What happened and what we're doing about it"

**SUPPORTED** for the assertion it is attached to (a 2025 model update was rolled back for
sycophancy).

Dated April 29, 2025:

> "We have rolled back last week's GPT-4o update in ChatGPT so people are now using an earlier
> version with more balanced behavior. The update we removed was overly flattering or
> agreeable—often described as sycophantic."

The preference-training link is also in the post:

> "We also teach our models how to apply these principles by incorporating user signals like
> thumbs-up / thumbs-down feedback on ChatGPT responses. However, in this update, we focused too
> much on short-term feedback, and did not fully account for how users' interactions with ChatGPT
> evolve over time. As a result, GPT-4o skewed towards responses that were overly supportive but
> disingenuous."

One framing note for the author, short of a reclassification: the post attributes the failure to
over-weighting short-term feedback **in that one update**, and describes it as something being
corrected, not as a property the training procedure cannot shed. The manuscript's "embedded
deeply enough in preference training" is a stronger reading than the post asserts, though the
bridge is the author's own inference and the empirical support for it is in sharma2024towards
(§4.3, the Claude 2 preference model preferring sycophantic responses over more truthful ones).

Internet Archive snapshot of 2025-05-01, which is the live page as published.

### Bibliographic anomalies noticed in passing
