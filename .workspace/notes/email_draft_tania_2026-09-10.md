**Subject:** Introduction, your version is in

Your version is in almost as written, and it is better than what it replaced. The concrete
opening, the question at the end of the first paragraph, Figure 1 as the motivating example
rather than an aside, and the harder gap sentence all stayed. So did naming the revision
tax, and the line about counting the initial draft, which fixes a turn-counting confusion
that had been costing us.

On your two open issues: both quote an abstract that is two versions old. The current one
already carries 70% and 27%, the same figures the introduction used, and it closes on
naming the fault reversing the effect rather than on revision robustness. So the mismatch
you found is not in the current pair. The worry underneath it was worth raising though,
because you asked whether the results section had gained the analysis or the introduction
was citing something unwritten. It gained the analysis. The direction result is now in
Results with its confidence interval and sign test.

Three things changed from your draft. The per-domain decline is negative in all five
domains but significant in four, with data logic marginal at p = 0.058, so that sentence
now says both. The 0.74 comes from the fifty trials that revise at every turn rather than
from all of them, so it now carries that basis and sits alongside the 70%, which covers all
718 revisions. And the blind-reader result is stated as a null, because the 65% bar was
pre-registered and not cleared; reading the null as evidence that the degradation is
invisible is an argument the Discussion can make but the introduction cannot.

One correction on the style evidence. A question by the end of the first or second
paragraph appears in five of his twenty-four introductions rather than in all of them, so
it is a move he uses rather than a rule. The bold holds: twenty-three of twenty-four.

The question I want your answer on, and only this one. Your second issue asked which claim
the paper is actually arguing for. The introduction now closes on the thesis, that intrinsic
self-correction requires extrinsic direction. The abstract closes on the finding, that
undirected revision lowers quality and naming the fault reverses it. Should both close on
the same one, and if so which?
