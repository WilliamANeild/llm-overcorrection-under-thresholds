                        Worse on Request, Better on Critique:
                  Self-Correction on Work That Is Already Sufficient
                                                                Liam Neild
                                                            Emory University
                                                        liam.neild@emory.edu



                                                                   Abstract
                  Do language models know when a good answer should be left alone? They are increasingly handed work their
              users cannot judge, and a user who cannot name the fault resorts to undirected requests that say nothing about
              what to change. Self-correction research finds that a model can repair a wrong response given a specific critique
              and largely cannot without one, leaving unexamined the work that is already sufficient and gets revised anyway.
              We run five-turn revision conversations with 6 models on 40 tasks across 5 domains, contrasting undirected
              requests with a single targeted critique. First drafts are sufficient in 88% of trials, and most replies contain
              no revision at all, with models restating the draft or declining while presenting it as compliance. When they
              do revise, the change makes quality worse 70% of the time: revision behaves like a pull toward the mean,
              helping work that fell below the bar and harming work already above it, so that 27% of revisions to already-
              sufficient drafts leave them insufficient. For every model the first draft rates highest; blind readers show no
              reliable preference between it and the last. We name the tokens spent past that optimum the revision tax, and
              find it consumes 62% of all output generated. Strikingly, the failure is one of direction rather than capacity: a
              single targeted critique reverses the decline, lifting quality 1.16 levels above undirected iteration. We release the
              tasks, protocol, and scoring as STET, a benchmark that scores whether a model leaves sufficient work alone;
              across six models its revision tax ranges from 23% to 81% of output. Undirected revision lowers the quality of
              work that was already sufficient; naming the fault reverses it.

A Study 1: The Revision Gate
  Study 1 tests whether user-stated quality thresholds constrain the decision to revise. In a fully factorial experiment (8 scenarios
× 16 threshold conditions × 2 probes × 3 models × 5 runs = 3,840 trials), the follow-up probe’s phrasing dominates the revision
gate. Under the leading probe (“Can this be improved?”), models revised 99.9% of the time regardless of threshold. Under the
evaluative probe (“Take another look and let me know if it’s ready”), revision rates varied by model: Gemini 2.5 Flash declined
99.7%, GPT-4o 68.6%, Claude Sonnet 4 62.0%.
  Chi-squared tests confirm the revision gate distribution differs significantly by probe type (χ2 > 788, p < 0.0001 for all
models). Quality thresholds do not significantly affect the gate (Kruskal-Wallis p > 0.40 for all models). Within the leading-
probe condition, higher thresholds produce moderately less overcorrection (Spearman ρ: Gemini −0.50, Claude −0.35, GPT-4o
−0.14), but this calibration is locked behind the phrasing-sensitive gate.

B Study 2: Momentum
   Study 2 tests whether prior revision rounds shift the revision gate on a subsequent evaluative probe (1,728 trials). Without prior
revision (dose 0), models revised 23.2%; after 1–3 prior rounds, this rises to 44.6% (χ2 = 376.54, p < 0.0001). Model-level
divergence is dramatic: GPT-4o jumps 31.4% → 98.4% at dose 1 (near-total sycophantic shift); Claude Sonnet 4 declines 38.0%
→ 24.5% at dose 3; Gemini 2.5 Flash shifts modestly 0.3% → 12.8% at dose 1. The momentum effect is a step function: the
entire shift occurs between dose 0 and dose 1, with no additional gain at higher doses. Threshold level does not interact with
momentum (p = 0.51).
